## 3\. Chess and Texas Hold'em

Both are two-player competitive games. Describe how hidden information and
randomness change what the player is actually doing in each. Then give one
concrete design consequence: something you would have to change if you moved
chess's rules to poker's information model, or the other way around.





Chess has perfect information because both players can see every piece and the entire board. There is no random deal, so players mainly make decisions by calculating possible moves and predicting their opponent's responses. Texas Hold'em has hidden cards and random card deals, so players must make decisions with incomplete information and estimate probabilities.



If chess used poker's information model, some pieces or their locations could be hidden, and players would need to make decisions without knowing the complete board. This would make deduction and uncertainty more important than simply calculating visible moves.



4\. Tetris

long, and what would that change cost?



People may get bored of Tetris after a long time because the main activity repeats: place falling pieces, clear lines, and try not to lose. The cost would be more complexity, which could distract from the simple and satisfying gameplay that makes Tetris work.

## 6\. Autobattler design

Dota Underlords and Hearthstone Battlegrounds are auto-battlers. Pick one
design rule -- for example, that a triple can only be ranked up once, or that
damage scales with the number of surviving units -- and explain the trade-off
it makes: what it encourages in the player, and what it costs.





One design rule in an autobattler is that damage can increase based on the number of surviving units at the end of a fight. This encourages players to build strong teams and keep units alive instead of only trying to win the current battle. The trade-off is that a player who loses a fight badly may also lose much more health, making it harder to recover and potentially causing games to end quickly.
